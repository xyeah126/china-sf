Lu Xun (1881–1936), pen name of Zhou Shuren, courtesy name Yucai, from Shaoxing, Zhejiang, is the founder of modern Chinese literature. He went to Japan in 1902 to study medicine, then abandoned it for letters. In 1903, in Tokyo, he rendered Jules Verne's From the Earth to the Moon from its Japanese version as A Trip to the Moon (Yuejie Luxing) and prefaced it with a manifesto for scientific fiction. Lamenting that Chinese fiction overflowed with romance, anecdote and the supernatural while scientific novels were as rare as a unicorn's horn, he called for fiction 'warped with science and wefted with human feeling', hoping readers would, all unawares, gain a little knowledge, break inherited superstition, improve their thought and assist civilization. He later translated Verne's Journey to the Centre of the Earth as well. Though Lu Xun never wrote science fiction himself, these translations and declarations introduced scientific fiction to China as a self-conscious genre, and he is universally credited as the starting point of Chinese science fiction.
