Chinese-American science fiction writer and translator, born in 1976 in Lanzhou, Gansu, who emigrated to the United States at eleven. He studied English literature and computer science at Harvard College and took a JD at Harvard Law School, working as a software engineer and later a high-tech litigation consultant before writing full time from 2017.

He debuted with the short story Carthaginian Rose in 2002, and a prolific period began around 2010. The Paper Menagerie (2011) is the only work of fiction ever to win the Hugo, Nebula and World Fantasy Awards — it uses living origami to tell of a Chinese-American boy and his mother, sundered by language and restored by memory. Mono no Aware took a second Hugo for Best Short Story in 2013, making him the only Chinese-heritage writer to win the award in consecutive years. Good Hunting won the 2016 Seiun Award for Best Translated Short Fiction in Japan and was adapted in 2019 as episode 8 of season 1 of Netflix's Love, Death & Robots. The Man Who Ended History: A Documentary was a finalist for the Hugo, Nebula and Theodore Sturgeon Memorial Awards.

Beyond short fiction, his first novel The Grace of Kings (2015) introduced the term silkpunk: bamboo, silk, ox sinew, coral and feather replace steampunk's brass gears, and machines run on biomechanical rather than thermodynamic principles. This is more than an aesthetic substitution — it re-signs the history of technology, restoring East Asian material culture, long filed under "ornamental" in Western-centred accounts, as a viable technical lineage. Built on the Chu–Han contention, the novel won the 2016 Locus Award for Best First Novel, was a Nebula finalist, and grew into a four-volume series.

His short fiction is collected in The Paper Menagerie and Other Stories (2016), winner of the 2017 Locus Award for Best Collection, and The Hidden Girl and Other Stories (2020). Several selections have appeared in Chinese, including The Algorithms for Love, The Killing Algorithms, The Shape of Thought, Transmigration Interface and Good Hunting.

For Chinese readers he is better known as a translator and editor. His English versions of Liu Cixin's The Three-Body Problem (2014) and Hao Jingfang's Folding Beijing (2016) won the Hugo Awards for Best Novel and Best Novelette; Liu Cixin called him "the bridge across two cultures and two stretches of time". He has translated more than forty stories by Chen Qiufan, Han Song, Xia Jia, Baoshu, Zhang Ran and Ma Boyong, and edited two anthologies of Chinese science fiction in translation, Invisible Planets (2016) and Broken Stars (2019). He remains the single most important conduit for Chinese science fiction into English.

He has notably declined to translate his own fiction back into Chinese, reasoning that writing genuinely native Chinese requires full immersion in the language's cultural context; he prefers to remain a "cultural coordinator" carrying Chinese work into English. The Chinese edition of his Chu–Han novel is therefore the work of another translator.

His work repeatedly takes up colonialism, language, memory and cultural translation, answering — politely but firmly — the Western genre's habit of projecting its own fears onto Chinese characters. Training in both law and programming means evidence, burden of proof and the question of who may speak for another's history recur as the skeleton of his fiction. He has said his guiding principle is that "humanity outweighs the science fiction"; readers in Chinese call him the "heartbreaker Liu" for the quiet devastation of his endings.
