A work in ten chapters by Wang Jia of the Eastern Jin, edited with added commentary by Xiao Qi of the Liang. The first nine chapters record marvels from the age of Fuxi and Shennong down to the Eastern Jin; the tenth is devoted to immortal mountains overseas. Its ornate prose stands in sharp contrast to the plainness of Gan Bao's In Search of the Supernatural, and it is the most literary of the Six Dynasties marvel collections.

It abounds in marvellous artefacts: the moon-piercing raft of Shun's reign that circles the moon once every twelve years with winged men aboard; the billow-sinking boat that travels beneath the sea without wetting its passengers; the wandering-immortal pillow, the bone-illumining gem and the dust-repelling rhinoceros horn, each described by form, material and effect.

Their value lies in their concreteness. The moon raft is not abstract immortals' art but a craft with a period, a crew and a return voyage; the boat is explicitly a submersible. They convert the mythic wish of ascending to heaven and entering the earth into describable mechanical designs.

The book is therefore one of the most important early documents of Chinese imagining of flying and submersible craft. Filed under Myth, though in its catalogue of marvellous machines its substance is already close to later technological fantasy.

Its later influence lies chiefly in the replicable catalogue of artefacts it supplied: descriptions of marvellous objects in later hagiographies, in the Dongming ji and in Tang tales mostly find their prototype here. For imaginative literature its chief contribution was to raise the marvellous artefact from an accessory of myth to an object that can be described and classified in its own right — a prerequisite for technological fantasy to exist at all.
