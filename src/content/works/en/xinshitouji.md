In forty chapters, written by Wu Jianren in 1908 and published by the Reform Fiction press under the name Old Youth. It is the most singular of the many sequels to Dream of the Red Chamber, sending Jia Baoyu into China at the start of the twentieth century.

The first half follows Baoyu through the modern world — concessions, newspapers, railways and steamships — defamiliarising the predicaments of late imperial China. The second half enters the Civilised Realm, an ideal state governed by the other Baoyu and advanced in technology: flying carriages, tunnel trains, submarines, bone-scopes and marrow-scopes, climate-controlled glass houses, and medicine capable of improving the human stock. There Baoyu observes an ideal society at work.

Its imagination is concentrated in the Civilised Realm. Though the machines are mostly explained by a catch-all appeal to electricity, their functions are concrete, their uses fully situated, and they are organised within an overall programme of social improvement — a rudimentary technological utopia.

Holding an ideal against reality, the novel voices its author's hope that artefacts might save the nation. Filed as SF, it is a representative work of Late Qing technological utopianism.

As a sequel to Dream of the Red Chamber the choice is singular: rather than continuing the emotional thread of Baoyu and Daiyu, it sends Baoyu into an entirely strange modern world. Defamiliarisation thus becomes the method of the whole book — seen through the eyes of a classical character, railways, newspapers and concessions reveal their own strangeness. The turn to the Civilised Realm in the second half applies the same method in reverse.
