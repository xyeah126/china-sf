A signature work by Wang Jinkang published around 1997, alongside Song of Life among his most important short fiction. Using virtual reality as its apparatus, it opens a philosophical enquiry into reality and perception.

The protagonist enters a nested virtual environment whose every layer is convincing enough to pass for the real; to know whether he remains in the actual world he must peel away the layers one by one. The word shell names both the virtual stratum and the limits of the senses.

Its imagination converts the classic brain-in-a-vat problem into a workable narrative structure: the seven layers are at once a plot mechanism and a graduated doubt about the reliability of perception.

Among the earliest systematic treatments of virtual reality in Chinese science fiction, its influence can be traced through much later work. Filed as SF.

The act of peeling is performed repeatedly in the story, and its effect diminishes with each repetition: every layer removed leaves less reason to trust the next. Wang thereby turns a technological premise into a test of the reader — on what grounds do we believe this is not another shell? Embedding the mechanism of doubt into the narrative structure gives the story an unsettling reading experience beyond the rigour of its premise.
