Published by Xiao Jianheng in 1962, this is his most widely read work. Xiao was one of the important pioneers of early Chinese science fiction, writing across several decades chiefly for children and young readers.

The story turns on a dog named Booker who is injured in an accident and given organ transplants by medical researchers, setting off a chain of consequences concerning life, memory and identity. Told from a child's perspective in a child's language, it renders complex medical speculation plainly.

Its imagination centres on transplantation: what is conceived is not merely the replacement of organs but the question of the continuity of the individual afterwards — is Booker, with new organs, still Booker? That question remains central to science fiction today.

A classic of children's science fiction in the People's Republic, it shaped the earliest scientific imaginings of generations of readers. Filed as SF.

Organ transplantation was a bold subject for early Chinese science fiction, touching both a medical technology then immature and the sensitive ground of ethics and identity. Xiao's method is to turn it into a question a child can grasp — whether Booker is still the same dog — giving a complex issue a clean point of entry. Replacing abstract discussion with a concrete question is among the most effective strategies of children's science fiction.
