A novel by Wang Jinkang published around 2000, imagining a social order in which human cloning has matured into an industry. Continuing the author's concern with the ethics of biotechnology, it moves the discussion from the individual to the institutional level.

It is set in an age when clones are manufactured at scale for labour, for organs and for particular services, and when law and ethics have evolved a whole apparatus of classification and management. The protagonist gradually discovers the contradictions and cruelties inside that order.

Its imagination lies in institutional design: the legal standing of clones, the means of identifying them, and the ways a society rationalises discrimination are all worked out in detail. This social extrapolation is what distinguishes Wang from other writers.

Among the most systematic treatments of cloning in Chinese science fiction. Filed as SF.

The novel's edge lies in showing how discrimination becomes institutional: society need not feel aversion to clones, only fix upon them a definition convenient to administer. Wang details how such a definition arises, how it is enforced, and how it is internalised as common sense. This depiction of how institutions generate themselves is rare in Chinese science fiction and lifts the book beyond the cloning question alone.
