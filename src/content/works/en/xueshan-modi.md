A fine short story by Tong Enzheng published around 1978, belonging with Mist in the Ancient Gorge to his line of archaeological science fiction. It weaves together the working methods of field archaeology, the natural environment of the Tibetan plateau, and an ancient legend.

An expedition discovers a ruin deep in the snow mountains and a flute of unknown origin; under particular weather and terrain the flute's sound is repeatedly triggered, and the team, following it, at last accounts for the phenomenon with acoustic and geological evidence.

Its imagination lies in reducing the supernatural to a natural mechanism: wind, ice and rock resonance are shown to be the material basis of the magic flute. This structure of explaining mystery by science is the most methodologically self-aware part of Tong's work.

Brief yet complete in atmosphere, it is among his best short pieces. Filed as SF.

The survey teams in Tong's fiction always respect the phenomenon first, look for an explanation second, and permit themselves a conclusion last. That procedural sense gives his work a particular persuasiveness: the marvel is credible because it is situated within a credible way of working. Using scientific method itself as the narrative skeleton was unusual in Chinese science fiction at the time and influenced a number of later writers.
